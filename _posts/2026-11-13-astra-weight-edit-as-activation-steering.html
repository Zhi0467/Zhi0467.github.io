In a widely circulated blog post Sparks of Weight-Space Programming, the authors highlight how GPT-6 Astra edits a small language model without any training: it writes a short program that changes parts of the MLP weight matrices to significantly improve the math performance of the model. The authors present this as evidence for a new paradigm they call Weight-Space Programming, stating that “neural network weights will soon be natively optimized by LLMs that deliberately reason about their weights.” The post, and especially its claim that frontier LLMs like Astra can “natively” edit weights, has received considerable attention. A single tweet by the lead author has been viewed nearly 10 million times (as of November 2026). While the authors and many commentators interpret this as “native weight editing,” we argue that Astra is acting like a good researcher, effectively implementing existing activation steering techniques, combined with a clever use of synthetic data. Additionally, recognizing the edit as activation steering allows us to further improve Astra's approach.

TL;DR

  • Astra’s edit of Qwen is equivalent to context-dependent activation steering: push the activation along a fixed direction constructed from data, with a strength linearly dependent on the current activations.
  • Training the "sensing vector", which adaptively changes steering strength depending on the context, can further improve the effectiveness of Astra's steering.
  • The clever synthetic data approach developed by Astra leads to better steering than using directions built from actual traces generated by Qwen.

1. From a “native weight edit” to activation steering

Astra’s procedure in the original blog is: inspect Qwen3.5-0.8B rollouts, identify looping, generate short synthetic pairs with and without repetition, construct a direction \(v\), and apply \(W'=W-\alpha vv^\top W\) to selected MLP output weight matrices.1

1neuron selection. This describes the earlier \(s=4v\) steering used as our baseline. Astra later selected 64 hidden neurons per layer; see the note below.

For the Qwen3.5-0.8B edit, we show that it is a special case of a known form of context-dependent activation steering, and that in some settings this view lets us improve on it.

There is already a discussion of how to understand “native weight editing” as described in the original blog post, including critical takes on its interpretation. Here we provide a formulation of the algorithm, experiments decoupling sensing from steering, and an examination of Astra’s clever use of synthetic data.2

2related discussion. See Matthew Honnibal’s account of the tools and feedback, Sean Goedecke’s commentary, and crabbix’s LessWrong post.

Formulation: activation steering

Take any block of a transformer, let the MLP’s hidden activations right before the output matrix be \(h\), its output weight matrix be \(W\), the residual stream \(r\), and this MLP's contribution to the residual stream be \(x=Wh\). Steering is the process of adding a vector to \(x\), \(h\), or \(r\) to elicit certain behaviors from the model. Consider the steered activation vector

\[ x' = x-(s^\top x)v. \]

There are two vectors here:

  • \(v\), called the steering direction, says where to push;
  • \(s\), called the sensing vector, the inner product \(s^\top x\) determines the steering strength given an input/context.

Here the strength depends on the output of the MLP. Because the strength is linear, this intervention is the same as a weight edit to the MLP output weight matrix (assuming no bias):

\[ W'=W-vs^\top W, \qquad W'h=Wh-v(s^\top Wh) = x'. \]

In this framing, Astra's edit on Qwen picks \(s = \alpha v\). In general, one is free to design both \(s\) and \(v\); in this post we focus on training \(s\), which further reduces looping.

We call this formulation activation steering, not to be confused with another form of steering which does \(x' = x - \gamma v\), where \(\gamma\) is a fixed number and context independent. The terminology is based on Contextual Linear Activation Steering (CLAS), Hsu et al., 2026, which trains a sensing vector.3

3literature attribution. The connection between activation and weight edits was established in Arditi et al., 2024 (weight orthogonalization being equivalent to directional ablation under certain conditions). Other relevant work includes Meng et al., 2022, Zou et al., 2023, and Wu et al., 2024.

2. Qwen's deficiency for math in the original blog is pathological

The headline claim is that Astra edits Qwen3.5-0.8B to make it better at math. What the edit actually does is stop the model from looping when thinking mode is turned on. This has the side effect of raising Qwen’s math scores, but it does not improve its underlying ability to do math. Admittedly, the original blog post mentions this but underemphasizes it.

We reproduce the experiment in Qwen3.5-0.8B’s thinking mode under the blog setting. Here “Astra’s steering” means \(s=4v\), with \(v\) built from Astra’s four synthetic pairs.4

Our evaluation uses the same problems as the original blog: 576 GSM8K training problems and 576 MATH training problems.

We note the steering as mainly fixing a pathological generation behavior, rather than eliciting new mathematical capability.

3. Training the sensing vector can improve on Astra’s steering

The goal of steering is to reduce the fraction of traces that loop, while accuracy, thinking closure, and cap hits are tracked as side effects. We show that training sensing vectors can further reduce looping compared to Astra’s steering. We use positive for non-loop text (called “productive” in the original blog) and negative for loop text.

The canonical steering method from the original blog sets \(s=\alpha v\). It uses the same direction to sense the context and to push/steer it. We keep \(v\) fixed and train \(s\), initialized at \(s = 0\) rather than \(v\), on a synthetic dataset. We compare next-token prediction (NTP), Corrective NTP and Unlikelihood (UL) training as defined below, with Astra's steering.

Specifically, let \(p_s\) be the steered model and \(p_0\) the original model. Define next token prediction loss, unlikelihood loss, and KL divergence:

\[ \begin{aligned} L_{\mathrm{NTP}}(s)&=-\mathbb{E}_{+}\log p_s(y_t\mid y_{<t}),\\ L_{\mathrm{UL}}(s)&=-\mathbb{E}_{-}\log\bigl(1-p_s(y_t\mid y_{<t})\bigr),\\ L_{\mathrm{KL}}(s)&=\mathbb{E}_{+}D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\left(p_0(\cdot\mid y_{<t})\,\|\,p_s(\cdot\mid y_{<t})\right). \end{aligned} \]

Here \(\mathbb{E}_{+}\) averages over positive tokens in the dataset; \(\mathbb{E}_{-}\) averages over loop tokens from the second copy of the repeated chunk onward. We average tokens within each trace, then average across pairs. Only \(s\) is optimized, model weights are frozen. For Corrective NTP, we apply the same next-token loss to a positive continuation following a loop prefix, denoting it \(L_{\mathrm{NTP}}^{\mathrm{corr}}\).

Based on these, we consider the following methods:

Method Training data and signal for \(s\) Objective
NTP Increase likelihood on original positive traces \(L_{\mathrm{NTP}}+\beta L_{\mathrm{KL}}\)
Corrective NTP Increase likelihood of positive continuations after loop prefixes \(L_{\mathrm{NTP}}^{\mathrm{corr}}+\beta L_{\mathrm{KL}}\)
Unlikelihood Decrease likelihood of repeated tokens in negative traces \(L_{\mathrm{UL}}+\beta L_{\mathrm{KL}}\)
\(s=\alpha v\) No sensing-vector training; choose \(\alpha\) on development data —

We use either Astra’s four original pairs or those four plus 28 additional hand-written pairs. Each pair consists of two rollouts, one is positive, the other negative (containing loops). These underlying pairs are used to construct \(v\) and used to supply the training text to learn \(s\). NTP uses the positive traces; Unlikelihood uses the negative traces. Corrective NTP places the positive continuation after varying amounts of repetition from its paired negative text. The supplementary methods give the construction details.

At each steered layer, \(v\) is the difference between the mean residual stream over the last 36 tokens of the negative text and that of the positive text, averaged over pairs and normalized to unit length. For \(s = \alpha v\) (Astra's method) we sweep \(\alpha\). Sensing vector training uses Adam with early stopping. Development data select hyper-parameters and early stop step.

Result: less looping with trained sensing vector

Figures 1a and 1b show each dataset separately. Figure 1c pools GSM8K and MATH, 1,152 test problems in total.

With 32 pairs, Unlikelihood training reduces the loop fraction from 27.2% with \(s=\alpha v\) to 6.5%; Corrective NTP reduces it to 10.9%. At four pairs, the corresponding fractions are 29.5%, 11.1% and 15.7%. Training \(s\) can do the intended job of fixing this pathological behavior better.

In this comparison, NTP on positive traces alone works poorly, while both Unlikelihood and Corrective NTP substantially reduce looping.

4. Where do steering vectors come from and where to sense?

Astra's short synthetic pairs are especially effective for steering direction

We compare two ways of constructing the steering vector, one by taking averaged difference of residuals on the synthetic pairs, the other on much longer natural rollout pairs. Setup: Qwen3-1.7B, greedy decoding, MLP-output steering at blocks 15–18, with the test prompts and the selection and stopping rules of Section 5. We compare directions from model-generated positive/loop responses against the 32 short synthetic pairs used in Section 5. The natural traces are the unedited model's own responses, 32 looping and 32 non-looping across both benchmarks, generated with a 40,960-token context limit (prompt plus completion). We retain the preceding rollout as context, but construct \(v\) from 36-token windows: the last 36 loop tokens for negative traces and the last 36 thinking tokens before thinking closes for positive traces. Sensing-vector training scores these selected windows too, not every token in the rollout. We test both \(s=\alpha v\) and a trained unlikelihood \(s\).6

6setup. For the specific setup, see reproduction details.

Overall, short synthetic pairs give much stronger loop reduction while accuracy stays roughly the same, which is a clever steering vector construction from Astra. Our tentative explanation: long responses differ in content, position and reasoning path as well as looping, adding unrelated variation to the activation contrast. Short synthetic pairs may isolate repetition more cleanly.

Where to sense: MLP output or residual stream?

We find that trained sensing vector can perform as well when transferred directly to sensing the residual, meanwhile, Astra’s \(s = \alpha v\) needs different \(\alpha\) for different sensing locations.

There are three sites worth distinguishing: MLP hidden activations before output \(h\), the MLP output \(x=Wh\), and the residual stream \(r\). Even when two interventions use the same \(v\) and \(s\),

\[ x'=x-v(s^\top x) \qquad\text{and}\qquad r'=r-v(s^\top r) \]

sense different quantities. The second sensing sees the accumulated stream: the embedding, every earlier attention and MLP output, and the corrections made by earlier steering interventions.7

7sensing location. This changes what the same \(s\) does. Take \(s=\alpha v\) with unit \(v\). At the output, each steering intervention multiplies the sensed coordinate of its own MLP's output by \(1-\alpha\), once, and leaves the rest of the stream alone. On the residual stream, each intervention acts on the accumulated stream. For an existing residual contribution, the direct effect of two steering interventions is \((I-\alpha v_2v_2^\top)(I-\alpha v_1v_1^\top)\). If both use the same unit direction \(v\), its coordinate along \(v\) is multiplied by \((1-\alpha)^2\). With different layer-specific directions, the product of projections replaces this scalar factor. At \(\alpha=1\) this is directional ablation of \(v\) (Arditi et al., 2024). For \(1<\alpha<2\) the coordinate flips sign and shrinks, and for \(\alpha>2\) it flips sign and grows.

We moved these two \(N = 32\) steering methods from the MLP output to the residual stream in the Astra setup, keeping \(v\) and \(s\) unchanged. We also recalibrated \(\alpha\) for the residual stream with \(\alpha\in\{0.2,0.5,1,1.5\}\), chosen on the development problems like every other setting. Development accuracy was 39.8%, 66.0%, 66.0% and 63.7%; of the two tied settings, \(\alpha=1\) loops less (3.5% against 12.5%) and was chosen. On the 1,152 test problems:

The sensing vector trained on the MLP output transfers to the residual stream with a similar benefit. The \(s=\alpha v\) method needs \(\alpha\) retuned. Recalibrated to \(\alpha=1\), it gives a similar benefit to the MLP-output steering methods: about the same accuracy, with loop rates as low as the trained sensing vector's.

5. A non-pathological setting shows loop reduction with similar accuracy

We show that steering can reduce looping in a non-pathological setting, though with less significant influence over accuracy.

  • either \(s = \alpha v\) or a trained sensing vector can reduce loop more than thinking off (except for N = 32 NTP).
  • Unlikelihood-trained sensing vectors reduce more loops than \(s = \alpha v\).
  • overall, this modestly shows activation steering as a way to reduce loop while retaining accuracy in a non-pathological setting.

The pairs for training \(s\) and building \(v\) are math-style text, yet the steering also helps suppress loops on code, loop suppression seems to transfer with these directions. Our development-selected settings have not found a trained sensing vector accuracy advantage here, but it still tends to reduce looping more than \(s = \alpha v\) which is the intended effect.

6. Conclusion

Astra’s weight edit can be understood as context-dependent activation steering. This view separates where to push from how strongly to push, and training the sensing vector can further reduce looping. The data used to construct the direction and train the sensing vector, and the site where it senses, all matter. Astra’s short synthetic pairs are a particularly effective choice in our comparison.

7. Notes

Reproduction details. The GitHub repository contains the code and experiment recipes. The supplement gives the protocols, including how we selected the Qwen3 test prompts; those prompts include known looping cases, so their loop rates are not benchmark-wide estimates.

Using short synthetic pairs is a clever choice. Astra’s short pairs and simple \(s=\alpha v\) are effective choices. Which data to use to construct \(v\) and train \(s\), where to sense, and what behavior the objective preserves are all consequential design choices. This also suggests a constructive role for agents: searching this design space, inspecting failures, and trying different training objectives and train/dev sets.

Steering across temperatures. Switching the Astra setup to greedy decoding without retuning sharply reduced development accuracy. Removing only the presence penalty had a smaller effect. The \(s\) and \(v\) that work under one temperature may need retuning under another; the decoding diagnostic gives the comparison.

Another axis: how to construct the steering direction. We focused on \(s\), but \(v\) is another vector to construct from data. A natural next comparison is to try mean differences of residuals, logistic regression, recursive feature machines, and PCA while keeping the sensing vector fixed.

Training the sensing vector has dataset-specific side effects. On GSM8K, the completion metrics move in the favorable direction numerically. On MATH, exact repetition falls dramatically, but more answers exhaust the budget and fewer close thinking. Some capped responses paraphrase instead of repeating exactly. Can we steer multiple behaviors at once? The dataset-level diagnostics show the breakdown.

Neuron selection in Astra’s final steering. Astra later restricted steering to 64 MLP neurons per layer. This version reaches 60.2% accuracy and 29.6% looping, compared with 57.7% and 29.5% for our earlier four-pair baseline. It senses selected hidden neurons rather than their combined MLP output. The supplement gives its equivalent formulation and separate random-direction control.

When to early stop sensing-vector training? We need a validation criterion, but what should it measure? Teacher forcing on saved development traces is cheap; generating fresh rollouts measures the accuracy, repetition, and completion we actually care about, but is much more expensive. Falling training loss did not reliably preserve thinking closure in our runs. The early-stopping diagnostic describes the proxies we tried and the closure check we used.
